## What interviewers are really asking

Almost every round — recruiter call, hiring manager, even a system design round — opens with some version of **"Tell me about yourself"** and soon moves to **"Walk me through a project you're proud of."** These feel like warm-ups. They are not.

- **"Tell me about yourself"** is the interviewer's first guess at your **level and fit**. In 90 seconds they decide which parts of your background to probe and, for recruiters, which team or level to route you to.
- **"Why are you looking to switch?"** checks whether you are moving *toward* something or running *from* something — and whether you'll say bad things about them later too.
- **"Why this company?"** checks whether you did homework and whether your motivation matches what the job actually offers.
- **"Walk me through a project"** is a disguised system design and ownership interview. The interviewer picks one thread — scale, a failure, a trade-off — and pulls until they find the edge of your understanding.

A useful analogy: your intro is the **API summary** — short, versioned for the caller. The project deep dive is the **design doc** — the interviewer reads it critically and opens comments on every part that seems hand-wavy.

> **How to say it in an interview (to yourself):** "The intro tells them where to dig. I should point them at the parts of my work I can defend best."

## The framework

### Part 1 — The 2-minute intro: Present → Past → Future

Use three beats. Aim for **60–90 seconds** spoken. Go up to two minutes only if they asked for a detailed walkthrough.

```
 PRESENT (~30s)          PAST (~25s)                 FUTURE (~20s)
 role, team, scope,  ─►  1–2 turning points that ─►  what you want next and
 one result with a       explain how you got here    why THIS role fits it
 number                  (not a job-by-job list)     (hand-off to them)
```

| Beat | Include | Leave out |
|---|---|---|
| **Present** | Role, domain, the systems you own, their scale, one result with a number. | A list of every technology you've touched. |
| **Past** | One or two moments that shaped you: a domain switch, a big project, first time leading. | College details (unless you are early career), every past employer in order. |
| **Future** | What you want next (scope, domain, type of problem) and a specific link to this role. | "Better package" or complaints about your current job. |

**Template for a backend engineer (fill in your own facts):**

> "I'm a backend engineer at [Company], on the [team] team. We own [the order-events platform / payments routing / search indexing], which handles [rough scale: events per day, peak requests per second]. Most of my work is in Java and Spring Boot, with Kafka for async flows and [MySQL / Cassandra / Redis] for storage. Recently I [led / built] [one project], which [result with a number].
>
> Before this I [spent N years at Company Y on Z]. That's where I moved from [feature work] into [distributed systems / platform work], mainly because of [a turning point: an incident, a migration, an ownership opportunity].
>
> Next, I'm looking for [bigger scale / deeper ownership of a platform / a specific domain]. Your team's work on [specific thing from the job description or engineering blog] is close to what I've been doing and where I want to go, which is why I applied."

Notice: one number in Present, one turning point in Past, one **specific** hook in Future.

**A 30-second version** for short recruiter calls:

> "Backend engineer at [Company], [N] years, currently owning [system] at [scale]. Recently [result]. I'm looking for [what next], and your [specific team / problem] fits that closely."

### Tailoring per company and per round

Keep the skeleton. Swap the **highlights**.

| Situation | Emphasise in Present / Past |
|---|---|
| Role is platform / infra | Scale, reliability work, on-call, migrations, internal tooling. |
| Role is product backend | Business impact, working with product managers, shipping speed, experiments. |
| Fintech / payments | Correctness, idempotency, reconciliation, audits. |
| Large tech company with a published rubric | Ownership and scope; hint at stories that map to their stated values. |
| Early-stage startup | Breadth, speed, comfort without process. |
| Recruiter round | Plain language, level signals (scope, team size, ownership). |
| Hiring manager round | Team-relevant systems, how you work with others. |
| Technical round | Systems, scale, your technical decisions — keep it shorter so they can start the problem. |

Rule of thumb: **read the job description and pick the two bullet points that match your work best. Make sure your intro hits both.**

### Part 2 — "Why switch?" / "Why are you leaving?"

The safe framing is **push → pull**: turn whatever pushes you out into what pulls you toward the new role. This is not lying. "My manager micromanages" usually really means "I want more ownership", and the second statement is the true goal.

| What you might feel (don't say it this way) | What you say |
|---|---|
| "My team is stuck maintaining legacy code." | "I've learnt a lot running the existing system. Now I want to work on building and scaling newer systems." |
| "I'm underpaid." | Keep comp out of this answer. Raise it only at the offer stage. |
| "I didn't get promoted." | "I'm looking for a role with larger scope and clearer ownership of a platform." |
| "My manager doesn't listen." | "I do my best work where engineers own design decisions end to end." |
| "There were layoffs / a reorg." | State it plainly in one sentence, no drama, then move to what you want next. |
| "I'm bored." | "I've grown into the current scope. I'm looking for the next harder problem: [specific]." |

**Structure:** two or three sentences. Start with something genuinely positive about your current role, then the pull.

> "I've had a good run at [Company] — I've owned [system] for [N] years and it's stable now. The problems I want to work on next, like [larger-scale event processing / multi-region], aren't on our roadmap for a while, and your team is working on exactly that."

**Special cases:**

- **Short tenure (under about a year).** Give the concrete reason once ("the team I joined was merged into another org and the role changed"), take responsibility for wanting a clean move, and don't over-explain.
- **Laid off.** Say so plainly. It is common and not a judgement on you. Then describe what you've been doing and what you want next.
- **Serving notice / already resigned.** Say when you'll be free. It is useful information, not a weakness.
- **Counter-offer risk.** Some interviewers ask, "If your company offers more, will you stay?" Answer honestly. If your reason is genuinely about scope, say so: "My reason for moving is the kind of work, which a raise wouldn't change."

### Part 3 — "Why this company?"

Give **two or three specific reasons**, each one tied to something about you.

Good reason types:

1. **The problem / technical scale** — "Your [search ranking / payments / logistics] platform has [a specific challenge you read about]."
2. **The product** — something you noticed as a real user.
3. **The team or way of working** — something you learnt from an engineering blog post, a talk, or a conversation with an engineer there.
4. **The stage of the company** — growing fast, a recent product launch, a platform rebuild.

Homework that takes about 30 minutes per company:

- Read the **job description twice** and note the systems and problems mentioned.
- Read the **last two or three engineering blog posts** or conference talks from the company.
- **Use the product** for 10–15 minutes.
- Look up the **team on LinkedIn** to understand its size and background.

Weak answers: "Great culture", "Big brand", "I want to learn from the best", "Good work-life balance". They could apply to anyone.

### Part 4 — The project deep-dive pitch

When asked "tell me about a project", use this seven-step arc. Think of it as a mini design review, told in **about three to four minutes**, then handed over for questions.

```
 1 CONTEXT ─► 2 ARCHITECTURE ─► 3 MY ROLE ─► 4 HARD PROBLEM ─► 5 TRADE-OFFS ─► 6 RESULTS ─► 7 WHAT I'D CHANGE
   why it        boxes & arrows     what I         the one problem    options I       numbers      honest
   existed       in 30 seconds      owned          worth the time     rejected & why  before/after reflection
```

| Step | What to say | Time |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Context** | Business problem and why it mattered. Who used it. Scale. | 20–30 s |
| **2. Architecture** | The main components and the data flow. Offer to draw it. | 30–45 s |
| **3. Your role** | Exactly what you owned versus what others built. | 15 s |
| **4. Hard problem** | One genuinely hard technical problem: ordering, consistency, a hot partition, backpressure, a migration with no downtime. | 60 s |
| **5. Trade-offs** | Two or three options and why you chose one. What you gave up. | 30–45 s |
| **6. Results** | Before → after numbers. Second-order effects. | 20 s |
| **7. What you'd change** | One or two honest improvements with reasons. | 20 s |

**Choosing the project:**

- **You owned real decisions in it.** Not "I implemented tickets".
- **It has at least one hard distributed-systems problem** you can talk about in depth.
- **It's recent enough** that you remember the numbers and failure modes.
- **It fits the role.** For a platform role, choose infra-heavy work; for a product role, show business impact.

**Template for a Kafka-based backend project (fill in your own facts):**

> **Context.** "Our [order service] wrote to MySQL and then called [N] downstream services synchronously. During sales, any slow downstream made checkout slow, and partial failures left data inconsistent."
>
> **Architecture.** "We moved to an event-driven design. The order service writes the order and an outbox row in the same transaction. A relay publishes outbox rows to a Kafka topic keyed by order ID. Downstream services consume independently and are idempotent using the event ID."
>
> **My role.** "I designed the outbox and relay, chose the topic partitioning, and led the migration of [two] consumers. Another engineer built [the dashboard / the other consumers]."
>
> **Hard problem.** "Ordering per order mattered — a 'cancelled' event must not be processed before 'created'. Keying by order ID gives per-partition order, but we also needed producer retries not to reorder, so we used the idempotent producer, and consumers committed offsets only after processing. Separately, [one consumer did a slow external call], so I [moved it to a separate consumer group with its own retry topic] so it couldn't block others."
>
> **Trade-offs.** "We considered CDC from the binlog instead of an outbox relay. CDC avoids polling but needed infra we didn't run yet, so we chose the outbox with polling and accepted [a few hundred ms] of extra delay."
>
> **Results.** "Checkout p99 went from [X] to [Y] during the next sale; partial-failure incidents dropped from [N per quarter] to [M]."
>
> **What I'd change.** "I'd add schema validation in CI from day one — we had one incident from an incompatible field change."

### Part 5 — Preparing for drill-down questions

After your pitch, the interviewer picks a thread. Prepare answers for these areas **before** the interview.

| Area | Typical questions | What to have ready |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | How many requests per second? Events per day? Data size? Peak vs average? | Rough numbers, and how you'd find exact ones. Back-of-envelope reasoning. |
| **Data model** | Why that database? What's the primary key? What indexes? | Schema sketch, main queries, access patterns. |
| **Failure modes** | What if Kafka is down? What if the consumer crashes mid-batch? What if the DB fails over? | For each component: what breaks, what the user sees, how it recovers. |
| **Consistency** | Can a user see stale data? Duplicates? Out-of-order events? | Where you accept eventual consistency and how you handle duplicates (idempotency keys, dedup tables). |
| **Metrics & alerts** | How did you know it worked? What do you alert on? | Two or three key dashboards, the main SLOs, an incident you caught. |
| **Incidents** | Tell me about an outage in this system. | One real incident: detection, mitigation, root cause, follow-up. |
| **Alternatives** | Why not X? (gRPC vs REST, Postgres vs Mongo, Kafka vs a simple queue) | The real reason you chose, including team and operational reasons. |
| **Scaling 10x** | What breaks first at ten times the load? | The bottleneck (a DB shard, a hot partition, a downstream rate limit) and your plan. |
| **Your part** | Which parts did you personally write? | Exact ownership; don't overclaim. |

Two honesty rules:

1. **If you don't know, say so and reason out loud.** "I don't remember the exact number; it was in the low thousands per second at peak. If I needed it, I'd check [the dashboard]." That is much better than a confident wrong number.
2. **Don't claim work you didn't do.** The drill-down will find it. It's fine to say "another team owned that part; my understanding is...".

### Part 6 — Questions to ask the interviewer

The last five minutes are yours. Use them to collect information you actually need to decide, and to show how you think. Prepare a few per round type.

**Hiring manager:**

- What would success look like for this role in the first six months?
- What is the biggest technical challenge the team faces this year?
- Is this a new position or a backfill? How has the team changed over the last year?
- How are technical decisions made — by the team, by a tech lead, through design reviews?
- How do you give feedback, and what does the promotion process look like here?

**Engineers / peers:**

- What does on-call look like? How often are you paged, and what kind of issues?
- What does the path from a merged PR to production look like?
- What's something that surprised you after joining?
- What's one thing you'd change about how the team works?

**Recruiter:**

- What are the remaining rounds, and who will I meet?
- What level is this role scoped at, and how is level decided?
- What is the expected timeline to an offer?

**Closing question (any round):**

- "Is there anything in my background that makes you unsure about my fit? I'd be glad to address it now."

Avoid: anything a quick search would answer, salary questions in technical rounds, and questions that sound like complaints about your current job.

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — Tailoring the same intro for two companies

The engineer: about five years of experience, Java and Kafka, currently on an order-events platform. (Template — use your own facts.)

**For a payments company:**

> "...I own the order-events pipeline, and a big part of that work has been **correctness under failure** — idempotent consumers, an outbox so we never publish an event for a rolled-back transaction, and reconciliation jobs. Payments needs exactly that kind of correctness thinking, which is why your [payment routing] role stood out."

**For a large consumer-internet company:**

> "...I own the order-events pipeline, which peaks at [N] events per second during sales. Most of my recent work has been **throughput and latency at peak** — partitioning, consumer scaling, backpressure. Your [notifications / feed] platform operates at a much larger scale, and that's the step up I'm looking for."

Same person, same job. Different emphasis.

### Example 2 — Turning a weak intro into a strong one

**Weak:**

> "I did my B.Tech in 2019 from [college]. Then I joined [Company A], where I worked on Java, Spring Boot, MySQL, Redis, Kafka, Docker, Kubernetes, AWS. Then I moved to [Company B] where I work on microservices. I'm looking for a change for better growth."

Problems: starts in the past, lists technologies without results, no scale, no specific reason for this company.

**Strong:**

> "I'm a backend engineer at [Company B], on the team that owns [order processing]. It handles [N] orders a day across [M] services; I own the async layer built on Kafka. Last quarter I [cut duplicate notifications to near zero by making consumers idempotent]. Before this I spent two years at [Company A] on [inventory APIs], which is where I got pulled into my first big migration and got hooked on distributed systems. I'm now looking for a team building platform-level event infrastructure, and your [streaming platform] job description reads very close to that."

### Example 3 — Handling a drill-down you weren't ready for

> **Interviewer:** "What happens if your outbox relay crashes after publishing to Kafka but before marking the row as sent?"
>
> **Strong answer:** "Then it republishes that row after restart, so downstream gets a duplicate. That's why every consumer dedups on the event ID — we keep processed IDs in [a table with a unique constraint / a cache with TTL]. So the system is at-least-once with idempotent consumers, not exactly-once. We accepted that because exactly-once across the database and Kafka would have needed much more machinery."

The answer names the failure, the effect, the mitigation, and the trade-off. That's the pattern.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading your resume aloud in order.** They have it. Tell them the story behind it.
- **Technology lists instead of results.** "Java, Spring, Kafka, Redis..." says nothing about your level.
- **No scale anywhere.** Give at least one number: events per day, requests per second, team size, services owned.
- **A generic future.** "Looking for growth" applies to everyone. Name the kind of problem.
- **Badmouthing your employer or manager.** The interviewer assumes you'll say the same about them.
- **Leading with salary as the reason to switch.** Even when it's part of the truth, keep it for the offer stage.
- **Picking a project you can't defend.** If you didn't own the design, you'll struggle with "why not X?".
- **Spending three minutes on architecture and nothing on the hard problem.** The hard problem and trade-offs are what get you hired.
- **Inventing numbers under pressure.** Approximate honestly and explain how you'd verify.
- **No questions at the end, or only "What's the work-life balance?"** It signals low interest.

## Practice questions

<Practice>

<QA q="What are the three beats of a strong intro and how long should it be?" level="easy">

**Present** (role, systems, scale, one result), **Past** (one or two turning points that explain your path), **Future** (what you want next and a specific link to this role). About 60–90 seconds; two minutes at most.

</QA>

<QA q="Why should your intro end with the Future beat?" level="easy">

It hands the conversation back to the interviewer with a natural next question ("so why us?") and shows you're moving toward something specific. Ending on the past leaves an awkward pause and no direction.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you answer: Why are you looking to switch? when the real reason is a difficult manager?" level="easy">

Translate push into pull: "I do my best work where engineers own design decisions end to end, and I'm looking for a team that works that way." Keep it to two or three sentences, start with something positive about your current role, and never criticise the manager.

</QA>

<QA q="What makes a Why this company answer credible?" level="medium">

Specifics only someone who did homework would know: a system or challenge from the job description, an engineering blog post or talk, an observation from using the product, or a conversation with an engineer there. Two or three such reasons, each tied to what you want, beat any number of generic compliments.

</QA>

<QA q="List the seven steps of a project deep-dive pitch." level="medium">

Context → architecture → your role → the hard problem → trade-offs → results → what you'd change. Aim for three to four minutes, then invite questions. Spend the most time on the hard problem and trade-offs.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you pick which project to pitch?" level="medium">

Choose one where you owned real decisions, which contains at least one hard technical problem you understand deeply, which is recent enough to remember numbers and incidents, and which matches the role (infra-heavy for platform roles, business impact for product roles).

</QA>

<QA q="The interviewer asks a scale number you don't remember. What do you say?" level="medium">

Give an honest range and show how you'd find it: "Peak was in the low thousands of events per second — I'd confirm on our Kafka throughput dashboard." Then reason from it if useful. Never invent a precise figure.

</QA>

<QA q="How should your intro change between a recruiter round and a technical round?" level="medium">

Recruiter: plain language, focus on level signals — scope, ownership, team size, what you want next. Technical round: shorter, more technical — systems, scale, your technical decisions — so the interviewer can move to the problem quickly.

</QA>

<QA q="In your Kafka project, the interviewer asks: What happens if a consumer crashes after processing a message but before committing the offset?" level="hard">

On restart the consumer resumes from the last committed offset, so that message is processed again — at-least-once delivery. The defence is idempotent processing: dedup on an event ID (unique constraint or processed-IDs table), or make the write naturally idempotent (upsert by key). Mention the trade-off: exactly-once across Kafka and an external database needs extra machinery, such as storing the consumed offset in the same database transaction as the write, or dedup on an event ID.

</QA>

<QA q="The interviewer asks: What would break first at ten times the load? How do you structure the answer?" level="hard">

Walk the data path and name the first real bottleneck with a reason: a single database primary for writes, a hot partition from a skewed key, a downstream rate limit, consumer count capped by partition count. Then give the fix and its cost (sharding, a better key, more partitions with rebalancing impact, caching, batching). Showing you know where the limits are is the point.

</QA>

<QA q="What questions would you ask a hiring manager, and why?" level="hard">

Questions whose answers change your decision: what success looks like in six months, the team's biggest technical challenge this year, whether the role is new or a backfill, how technical decisions get made, and how feedback and promotion work. They reveal priorities, team health and growth, and show you're evaluating fit seriously.

</QA>

<QA q="Scenario: You left your last job after eight months. How do you answer why?" level="scenario">

Give one concrete, verifiable reason without blame ("the product I joined for was shut down in a reorg and I was moved to unrelated work"), take ownership of wanting a clean move, and link to why this role is a stable long-term fit. Keep it short. Over-explaining sounds defensive.

</QA>

<QA q="Scenario: Mid-deep-dive, the interviewer says your design seems over-engineered. How do you respond?" level="scenario">

Don't get defensive. Restate the constraint that drove the design ("we had five downstream consumers and frequent partial failures"), acknowledge the cost ("it added a relay and a dedup table to operate"), and say what you'd simplify given different constraints. If they have a point, say so: "With hindsight, for two consumers a synchronous call with retries would have been enough."

</QA>

<QA q="Scenario: The interviewer asks whether you'd stay if your current employer makes a counter-offer." level="scenario">

Answer honestly. If your reasons are about scope or type of work, say a raise wouldn't change them. If money is part of it, you don't need to volunteer it here, but don't make promises you won't keep. Saying "I wouldn't be interviewing unless I were serious about moving" is fair if it's true.

</QA>

</Practice>

## TL;DR cheat sheet

- **Intro = Present → Past → Future**, 60–90 seconds, one number in Present, one specific hook in Future.
- **Tailor the highlights, not the skeleton.** Match the two most relevant job-description bullets.
- **Why switch:** two or three sentences, positive about the current role, push turned into pull. No blame, no salary.
- **Why this company:** two or three specific reasons from real homework (JD, eng blog, product, people).
- **Project pitch:** context → architecture → your role → hard problem → trade-offs → results → what you'd change. Three to four minutes.
- **Drill-down prep:** scale numbers, data model, failure modes, consistency, metrics, a real incident, alternatives, the 10x bottleneck, exactly what you owned.
- **Honesty:** approximate and explain how you'd verify; never claim work you didn't do.
- **End with good questions:** success in six months, biggest challenge, decision-making, on-call, team changes.
