## Definition (interview-ready)

**Salary negotiation** is the cooperative step after an offer where you and the company agree on a package that fits the level, without damaging the relationship you will work inside. Companies expect it.

It has three parts. **First, understand the offer.** Headline CTC (cost to company) mixes fixed pay, employer PF and gratuity, a target variable bonus, one-time bonuses and equity. ESOPs are options you must pay a strike price to exercise. RSUs are shares delivered as they vest, usually over four years with a one-year cliff. **Second, compare offers year by year** for four years, using realistic bonus payouts and heavily discounted private-company equity. **Third, negotiate well.** Avoid naming a number early (ask for the band instead) and never lie about current pay. Ask for the full offer in writing, then make one specific, justified, package-wide ask with a conditional close. Use only real competing offers, and honour your yes.

**One-liner:** Negotiation is a cooperative, honest exchange: understand every component of the offer, compare four-year real value rather than headline CTC, then make one specific, justified ask across the whole package and keep your word once you accept.

## What interviewers are really asking

By the time compensation comes up, the company has spent many engineer-hours interviewing you. A recruiter's job is to **close the hire within a budget**. Your job is to **reach a fair package without damaging the relationship you will work inside for years**. That is a cooperative problem with some tension in it, not a fight.

At each stage the questions mean something different:

- **"What are your expectations?"** (early) — "Help me check you're within the band before we invest more time." It is also an anchor: a low number here can cap your offer.
- **"What is your current CTC?"** — "Give me a reference point so I can offer a percentage hike on it." Useful to them; often not useful to you.
- **"Can you accept today?"** — "I want to close before a competitor does."
- **"This is our best offer."** — Sometimes literally true, sometimes a test.

The well-known guides on this topic — Patrick McKenzie's essay *Salary Negotiation* and Haseeb Qureshi's two-part series — agree on the core idea: **companies expect negotiation, information is leverage, and staying positive and honest keeps the deal alive**. This note turns that into a practical plan for a software engineer in India, without quoting salary numbers that go stale fast. For current market data, use crowd-sourced sites like levels.fyi and conversations with people in the role.

> **Mindset in one line:** "I'm excited about this role, and I want us to land on a package that reflects the level. Let's solve that together."

## The framework

### Step 1 — Know what an offer is made of

An engineering offer in India usually has several parts. Many offer letters lead with **CTC (Cost to Company)**: the total the employer expects to spend on you in a year. CTC is not your take-home pay.

| Component | What it is | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed / base** | Guaranteed annual salary, split into basic, HRA and allowances. | The **basic** share matters: employer PF and gratuity are calculated on the statutory wage base (basic plus DA). Under India's Labour Codes, in force since November 2025, excluded allowances above half of total pay are added back into that wage base. Gratuity already uses the new base; how PF moves over depends on rules still being notified. Ask HR how your structure is computed. |
| **Employer PF and gratuity** | Employer's provident-fund contribution and a gratuity provision, often shown inside CTC. | They are real benefits but not monthly cash. Gratuity is payable only after a qualifying period of service. |
| **Variable / performance bonus** | A target amount, often a percentage of fixed, paid annually or half-yearly depending on company and personal rating. | Is the target guaranteed in year one? What has the **actual** payout been historically? Is it pro-rated if you join mid-year? |
| **Joining (sign-on) bonus** | One-time cash. Used to compensate what you lose by leaving (an unpaid bonus, unvested stock) or to bridge a gap. | **Clawback**: do you repay it if you leave within 12 months (sometimes longer)? Is it paid upfront or with your first salary? |
| **Relocation** | Lump sum or reimbursed costs for moving cities. | Taxable treatment, clawback, temporary housing. |
| **ESOPs (stock options)** | The **right to buy** company shares later at a fixed **exercise (strike) price**. Common at startups and unlisted companies. | Strike price, how many options, vesting schedule, **exercise window after you leave**, how the company has provided liquidity (buybacks, secondary sales, IPO). |
| **RSUs (restricted stock units)** | A promise of actual **shares** delivered as they vest. Common at listed companies, including Indian subsidiaries of global companies. | Number of units or grant value, vesting schedule, which share price was used to convert value into units. |
| **Refreshers** | Additional equity grants after joining, often yearly and linked to performance. | Are they typical? This matters a lot once your initial grant is fully vested. |
| **Retention bonus** | Cash paid if you stay for a set time. | Conditions and clawback. |
| **Benefits** | Health insurance (and for whom), meal cards, learning budget, leave policy. | Usually standard company-wide, so rarely negotiable — but they affect real value. |

**ESOPs versus RSUs, in plain words:**

- An **ESOP** is like a coupon that lets you buy a share at yesterday's price. It is only worth something if the share's value is above your strike price **and** you can eventually sell. You usually have to **pay the strike price to exercise**, and in India the gain at exercise (fair market value minus what you paid) is taxed as a salary perquisite (employees of certain eligible startups can defer paying this tax), with capital-gains tax later when you sell. So exercising can cost real cash before you can sell anything.
- An **RSU** is a share delivered to you on the vesting date. It has value as long as the share price is above zero. In India the value at allotment is taxed as a perquisite, and any further gain is taxed as capital gains on sale.
- Tax law changes: the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026, and section numbers and rules have moved. **Check the current rules with a tax professional** before you model after-tax value.

### Step 2 — Understand vesting

**Vesting** means you earn the equity over time, not on day one.

```
  Common pattern: 4-year schedule with a 1-year cliff

  Month:  0 ────── 12 ─────── 24 ─────── 36 ─────── 48
  Vested: 0%       25%        50%        75%        100%
          └ cliff ┘ then monthly or quarterly in equal steps
```

- **Cliff**: nothing vests until the cliff date (often one year). Leave a day before it and you get nothing.
- **Even vs back-loaded**: some companies vest evenly; others give a small share in the early years and most in the later years. Amazon is the most widely reported back-loaded example, with larger cash joining bonuses in the first years to smooth total pay. **Read the exact schedule in your own grant letter**, because terms vary by company, year and level.
- **Grant value vs shares**: a grant stated as a value (for example "worth X over four years") is converted to a number of units at some share price. If the stock moves later, your real value moves with it.
- **Refreshers** keep total pay from dropping after year four. Ask how refreshers typically work.

### Step 3 — Compute the real value of an offer

Compare offers **year by year for the first four years**, not by headline CTC.

```text
For each offer and each year (Y1..Y4):

  cash        = fixed
              + variable × (realistic payout %, not the target)
              + joining bonus  (Y1, sometimes Y2)
              + relocation     (Y1)

  equity      = value of shares or options vesting that year
                (RSUs: units vesting × a conservative share price)
                (ESOPs: options vesting × (share value − strike) × your discount for risk and illiquidity)

  exclude     = employer PF/gratuity counted in CTC (note separately as long-term benefit)

  total_year  = cash + equity
```

Then:

- **Discount private-company equity heavily.** Unlisted shares have no market; their value depends on a future buyback, secondary sale or IPO. Treat them as upside, not salary.
- **Watch year one.** Joining bonuses make year one look large. Year two can fall sharply if the bonus was one-time and vesting is back-loaded.
- **Compare levels, not just money.** A higher level usually means more scope, a higher band at the next raise, and a better anchor for your next job.
- **Count the non-money parts.** Manager, team, learning, stability, commute or remote policy, on-call load. A small pay difference rarely outweighs a much better team.

### Step 4 — When to share expectations

The general advice (McKenzie and Qureshi both make this point) is to **avoid naming a number first, and especially to avoid naming it early**. Before the company has decided it wants you, any number you give works against you: too high and you may be screened out; too low and you've capped the offer.

**Early in the process (recruiter screen):**

> "I'd like to understand the role and level first. I'm sure we can reach a fair number if it's the right fit. Could you share the band for this level?"

**If they insist on a number:**

- Give a **researched range** based on market data for the role and level (levels.fyi, people you trust in similar roles), not your current pay.
- Make the bottom of your range a number you'd genuinely be happy with.
- Talk about **total compensation**, not just fixed.

> "Based on what I've seen for this level in the market, I'm looking at total compensation in the range of [A] to [B], depending on the split between fixed, variable and equity."

### Step 5 — Handling "What is your current CTC?"

This question is very common in India. Many companies also ask for **payslips** or verify your last salary during background verification (BGV). That shapes your options:

1. **Never lie** about current pay. BGV can catch it, and offers can be withdrawn.
2. **You can decline to make it the anchor.** Redirect to the role:
   > "I'd prefer to base this on the scope of the new role and the market for it, rather than my current package. For this level my expectation is around [range]."
3. **If you do share, share the full picture**, not just fixed pay: variable, unvested equity you'd give up, pending bonus, and any expected increment.
   > "My current fixed is [X]. On top of that I have a variable component of [Y], and [Z] in unvested RSUs that I'd forfeit by leaving. My increment cycle is in [month]."
4. **Frame expectations around the new role, not a hike percentage.** "A 30% hike on current" anchors on the wrong thing. "The band for this level" anchors on the right thing.

### Step 6 — Get the offer, then slow down

When the verbal offer comes:

1. **Thank them and show enthusiasm.** Qureshi's rules stress staying positive: a candidate who sounds keen is someone they will fight to close.
2. **Ask for the full breakdown in writing** — every component, vesting schedule, clawback terms, joining date, notice-period support.
3. **Don't accept on the call.** Ask for time.
   > "Thank you, I'm really excited about this. Could you email me the full breakdown? I'd like a couple of days to go through it with my family, and I'll come back to you by [day]."
4. **Keep notes.** Confirm important verbal points by email ("Just to confirm what we discussed: the joining bonus has a 12-month clawback, and you'll cover the notice buyout up to [X]"). McKenzie and Qureshi both recommend getting terms in writing.

**Exploding offers** (accept within 24–48 hours or lose it): ask for more time politely and explain why — you're finishing other processes or need to review with family. Qureshi reports that in his own job search, every exploding offer was extended once he politely refused the short deadline. A company that refuses any reasonable time is giving you information about how it treats people.

### Step 7 — Make your ask

Your counter should be **specific, justified and package-wide**.

- **Specific**: name the components and numbers you want. Vague asks ("can you do better?") get small moves.
- **Justified**: give a reason for each ask — the level's market range, a competing offer, equity or bonus you're forfeiting, the scope discussed in interviews.
- **Package-wide**: if fixed pay is tight because of bands, other levers often have separate budgets: joining bonus, equity, relocation, start date, level.
- **Conditional close**: say what would make you sign. This shows you're serious and gives the recruiter something concrete to take to the approver.

**Email script:**

```text
Subject: Offer discussion — [Your name], [Role]

Hi [Recruiter],

Thank you for the offer and for walking me through it. I'm genuinely excited
about the [team / problem] and the people I met during the interviews.

Having looked at the full package, there are two areas I'd like to discuss:

1. Fixed pay: based on market data for this level and the scope we discussed
   (owning [platform / area]), I was expecting fixed pay closer to [X].
2. Joining bonus: by leaving in [month] I forfeit [my annual bonus / Y in
   unvested RSUs vesting in month Z]. A joining bonus of [Y] would cover that.

If we can get to [X] fixed and a [Y] joining bonus, I'm ready to accept and can
confirm my joining date of [date].

Looking forward to working this out together.

Best,
[Name]
```

**Call script (if they phone you):**

> "Thanks for calling. First, I'm really keen on this role — the [team / problem] is exactly what I want to work on. I've gone through the offer carefully. The main gap for me is [fixed pay / equity]. For this level, and considering [competing offer / forfeited bonus], I was hoping for [X]. If you can get there, I'm happy to sign this week."
>
> Then **stop talking.** Let them respond. Silence is uncomfortable; filling it usually means negotiating against yourself.

**Typical responses and replies:**

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Fixed is at the top of the band." | "Understood. Could we look at a joining bonus or more equity to close the gap instead?" |
| "We don't do joining bonuses." | "Okay. I'm forfeiting [X] by leaving now — could equity or a revisited start date help cover it?" |
| "This is our best and final." | "I appreciate you pushing for this. Let me take a day to consider it properly." (Then decide honestly.) |
| "We can do [half of what you asked]." | "Thank you, that helps. If we can also [move one smaller item], I'll accept today." |
| "Can you decide by tomorrow?" | "I want to give a considered answer on something this important. I'll get back to you by [specific day]." |

### Step 8 — Use competing offers ethically

Competing offers are the strongest lever because they are evidence of your market value. Both Qureshi and McKenzie treat your alternatives (your **BATNA** — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement) as the source of leverage.

Ethical rules:

1. **Never invent or inflate an offer.** Recruiters may ask for the letter. Being caught ends the conversation and damages your reputation in a small industry.
2. **Only negotiate with companies you'd actually join.** Collecting offers purely as leverage wastes everyone's time.
3. **You don't have to share every detail.** It's fine to say "I have another offer at a higher total compensation" without sharing the letter, unless you choose to.
4. **Mention alternatives as context, not threats.** "I have another offer I'm considering, but this team is my first choice. Is there room to close the gap?" works far better than "Match it or I walk."
5. **Don't accept multiple offers and drop out later.** It's a known problem in Indian hiring: companies plan headcount around your acceptance. If you must back out, tell them immediately and honestly.
6. **Once you say yes, mean it.** Qureshi's advice: once you commit, honour it.

Timing: try to **bring offers to the decision point together**. Ask slower companies to speed up ("I have an offer with a deadline of [date]; is there any way to expedite the remaining rounds?") and faster ones for a little more time.

### Step 9 — Notice period and buyouts

Notice periods in Indian tech are often long, and they matter in negotiation because **the hiring company wants you sooner**.

- **What it is**: the period you must work after resigning, as written in your appointment letter.
- **Early release options**: your current employer may release you early, or allow you to "buy out" unserved days — usually by paying or forgoing salary for those days, recovered in your full-and-final settlement. The exact formula (basic, gross or CTC per day) depends on your contract and HR policy.
- **Buyout reimbursement**: some hiring companies will reimburse the buyout to get you earlier, often as part of or alongside the joining bonus. **Ask, and get it in writing**, including any clawback.
- **Relieving letter**: leaving without serving notice or settling dues can delay your relieving letter, which some new employers ask for. Check your contract before deciding.
- **Tax**: treatment of buyout amounts can be complex; confirm with a tax advisor.

Script:

> "My notice period is [N] days. I'll ask for an early release, but if a buyout is needed, could [Company] cover it so I can join by [date]?"

### Step 10 — Counter-offers from your current employer

When you resign, your current company may offer a raise, promotion or retention bonus to keep you.

Questions to ask yourself before considering it:

- **Why did I start looking?** If the reason was scope, team, manager or growth, more money changes none of it.
- **Why did it take a resignation?** If they could pay more now, why not before?
- **What changes in how I'm seen?** Some teams may treat you as a flight risk afterwards, while others genuinely value your stay. Judge your own situation.
- **What are the strings?** Retention bonuses often have clawbacks or lock-in periods.

Ethically: **don't use a counter-offer to reopen an offer you already accepted**, and **don't interview elsewhere only to get a raise** unless you're truly prepared to leave. If you do accept a counter-offer, inform the new company quickly and respectfully.

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — Comparing two offers (structure only)

Two hypothetical offers for the same engineer. Letters stand in for numbers so you can plug in your own.

| | Offer A (listed company) | Offer B (late-stage startup) |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | F_A | F_B (higher than F_A) |
| Variable | Target V_A; history shows close to target | Target V_B; history unknown |
| Joining bonus | J_A, 12-month clawback | None |
| Equity | RSUs, 4 years, 1-year cliff | ESOPs, 4 years, 1-year cliff, strike price S |
| Liquidity | Shares sellable after vesting | Depends on buybacks or an IPO; no market today |
| Notice buyout | Covered | Not covered |
| Level | One level higher than current | Same level |

How to reason:

- **Year 1 cash**: B's higher fixed is offset by A's joining bonus and notice buyout. Compute both.
- **Years 2–4**: A's RSUs have a market price; B's ESOPs need a heavy discount, and exercising them costs cash and triggers tax.
- **Level**: A's higher level improves future raises and your next job's anchor.
- **Then the non-money questions**: which manager and team did you trust more, and which problem do you want to work on?

Often, an offer with lower fixed pay but liquid equity and a higher level wins on four-year value. It depends on the numbers — which is exactly why you write them down.

### Example 2 — Negotiating without a competing offer

> "Thank you again for the offer — I'm excited about the role. I've researched what engineers at this level are offered for similar scope, and I was expecting the fixed component closer to [X]. I'm also leaving behind [a bonus / unvested equity] by moving now. Could we look at moving fixed to [X], or bridging the gap with a joining bonus?"

No competing offer needed: the justification is market data plus what you forfeit.

### Example 3 — The current-CTC question in the first recruiter call

> **Recruiter:** "What's your current CTC and expected CTC?"
>
> **You:** "My current package is [X] fixed, plus variable and unvested stock — I can share a full breakdown later. For expectations, I'd rather base it on the scope of this role. Could you tell me the band for this level? If it lines up, I'm confident we'll find a number that works."
>
> **Recruiter:** "We need a number to proceed."
>
> **You:** "Understood. For this level, I'm targeting total compensation in the range of [A] to [B], depending on how it's split between fixed, variable and equity."

## Common mistakes

- **Accepting on the spot.** You lose your one moment of leverage. Always ask for time and the full breakdown.
- **Anchoring on a hike percentage.** "30% over current" ties you to your old employer's pay, not the new role's band.
- **Negotiating only fixed pay.** Joining bonus, equity, level, start date and buyout often have more room.
- **Comparing headline CTC.** CTC includes PF, gratuity and target variable, and hides vesting shape. Compare four-year cash plus realistic equity.
- **Valuing startup ESOPs at face value.** Unlisted equity can be worth a lot or nothing. Discount it and check the exercise window and buyback history.
- **Ignoring clawbacks and cliffs.** A joining bonus you must repay, or a cliff you'll miss, changes the real value.
- **Bluffing a competing offer.** Easy to check, costly when caught.
- **Being aggressive or going silent.** Ultimatums make recruiters stop fighting for you; silence makes them assume you're gone. Keep communicating, warmly.
- **Lying about current pay.** Background verification can expose it and lead to a withdrawn offer.
- **Accepting multiple offers and ghosting.** Damages your reputation and the people who planned around you.
- **Optimising only for money.** A slightly bigger number with a bad manager or no growth is a bad trade over four years.

## Practice questions

<Practice>

<QA q="What is the difference between CTC and in-hand salary?" level="easy">

CTC is the employer's total yearly cost for you: fixed pay, employer PF, gratuity provision, target variable, sometimes insurance and one-time bonuses. In-hand is what reaches your bank account monthly: gross salary minus your PF contribution, income tax (TDS) and professional tax. Variable and one-time bonuses arrive separately, and employer PF and gratuity are long-term benefits, not monthly cash.

</QA>

<QA q="What is the difference between ESOPs and RSUs?" level="easy">

ESOPs are options: the right to buy shares at a fixed strike price later. They are worth something only if share value exceeds the strike and you can sell, and exercising costs cash. RSUs are shares delivered on vesting with no purchase price. ESOPs are common at startups; RSUs at listed companies. Both are taxed in India as a salary perquisite (ESOPs at exercise, RSUs at allotment) and then capital gains on sale.

</QA>

<QA q="What is a vesting cliff?" level="easy">

A period, often one year, before which nothing vests. At the cliff a chunk vests at once, then the rest vests in regular steps. Leaving before the cliff means forfeiting the entire grant.

</QA>

<QA q="Why should you avoid naming a number early in the process?" level="medium">

Before the company has decided it wants you, a number can only hurt: too high and you may be screened out, too low and you've capped the offer. After they've decided, they've invested in you and have more flexibility. Prefer asking for the band; if pressed, give a researched total-comp range based on the market for that level.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you answer the current CTC question in India without hurting your offer?" level="medium">

Don't lie, since background checks can verify it. Prefer to anchor on the new role's band. If you share, give the full picture — fixed, variable, unvested equity you forfeit, upcoming increment — and state expectations as a range for the new level rather than a hike percentage.

</QA>

<QA q="What should you check about a joining bonus?" level="medium">

The amount, when it's paid (upfront or with first salary), the clawback terms (repay if you leave within a period), whether it's split over two years, its tax treatment, and whether it's meant to cover a notice buyout or forfeited bonus.

</QA>

<QA q="Why can year two of an offer pay less than year one?" level="medium">

Year one often includes one-time cash — joining bonus, relocation. If equity vesting is back-loaded or starts after a cliff, year two may have no bonus and only modest vesting. Always model each of the first four years separately.

</QA>

<QA q="How would you value startup ESOPs in an offer comparison?" level="medium">

Start from options vesting per year × (estimated share value − strike), then discount heavily for risk and illiquidity. Check the exercise window after leaving, any buyback history, and the cash and tax needed to exercise. Treat the result as upside, not salary.

</QA>

<QA q="Write a two-sentence counter-offer ask." level="hard">

"I'm excited about the role and would like to close this quickly. Based on market data for this level and the [X] bonus I forfeit by leaving now, if we can reach [fixed X] with a [Y] joining bonus, I'm ready to accept this week." Specific, justified, package-wide, with a conditional commitment.

</QA>

<QA q="The recruiter says fixed pay is at the top of the band. What next?" level="hard">

Accept the constraint and move to other levers: joining bonus, more equity, relocation, notice-period buyout, a later start date, or a written early review for level or pay. Separate budgets often have room even when the band doesn't. Also check whether the level itself is right, since a higher level carries a higher band.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you use a competing offer ethically?" level="hard">

Only if it's real and from a company you'd actually join. Say it plainly and positively ("I have another offer at a higher total, but this team is my first choice — can we close the gap?"). Don't inflate it, don't threaten, and you don't have to share the letter. Once you accept, honour it.

</QA>

<QA q="Scenario: You get an offer with a 24-hour deadline while two other processes are in final rounds." level="scenario">

Thank them, express genuine interest, and ask for a specific extension with a reason: "I'm in final rounds elsewhere and want to make a considered decision — could I have until [date]?" Meanwhile ask the other companies to expedite. If they refuse any reasonable extension, weigh that as information about the company. Don't accept and plan to renege.

</QA>

<QA q="Scenario: Your notice period is 90 days and the new company wants you in 30." level="scenario">

Ask your current employer about early release or a buyout under your contract. Ask the new company to reimburse the buyout (in writing, with any clawback), possibly as part of the joining bonus. Make sure you'll receive a relieving letter, and confirm the tax treatment. Agree a realistic joining date with both sides.

</QA>

<QA q="Scenario: After you resign, your manager offers a big raise to stay. How do you decide?" level="scenario">

Go back to why you started looking. If it was scope, team or growth, money won't fix it. Ask why the raise needed a resignation, check for strings like retention clawbacks, and consider how you'll be seen afterwards. If you've already accepted the new offer, don't use the counter to renegotiate it. If you do stay, tell the new company immediately and respectfully.

</QA>

</Practice>

## TL;DR cheat sheet

- **Negotiation is expected and cooperative.** Stay warm, specific and honest.
- **Know the parts**: fixed (and basic), variable (realistic payout), joining bonus (clawback), relocation, ESOPs (strike, exercise window, liquidity), RSUs (vesting), refreshers, benefits.
- **Vesting**: often 4 years with a 1-year cliff; some companies back-load. Read your own grant letter.
- **Value offers year by year** for four years; discount unlisted equity; watch the year-two drop; compare levels.
- **Expectations**: ask for the band first; if pressed, give a researched total-comp range for the level.
- **Current CTC**: never lie; share the full picture if you share; anchor on the new role, not a hike percentage.
- **After the offer**: thank, ask for it in writing, take time, confirm verbal terms by email.
- **The ask**: specific + justified + package-wide + conditional close. Then stay quiet.
- **Competing offers**: real, positive, never inflated; honour your yes.
- **Notice period**: ask for early release; ask the new company to cover the buyout in writing; secure the relieving letter.
- **Counter-offers**: money rarely fixes the reason you started looking.
