## Definition (interview-ready)

A **Kafka consumer** is a client that repeatedly calls `poll()` to fetch records from the partitions it owns, processes them, and **commits offsets** to record progress. Consumers sharing a `group.id` form a **consumer group**; a broker-side **group coordinator** tracks membership, and whenever membership or subscriptions change it triggers a **rebalance** that redistributes partitions. Everything interesting about consumers — duplicates, stalls, lag spikes, lost messages — comes from three things: *when you commit*, *how long you take between polls*, and *how rebalances move partitions around*.

**One-liner:** A Kafka consumer polls records from the partitions its group assigns it and commits offsets to track progress; when members join or leave, a rebalance moves partitions, so you commit after processing, keep handlers idempotent, and use cooperative rebalancing plus static membership to keep rebalances cheap.

## Why it matters

Producers are mostly configure-once. Consumers are where production incidents live:

- A handler that occasionally takes 6 minutes gets the consumer kicked out of its group, its partitions handed to someone else, and the same batch reprocessed — forever.
- A rolling deploy of 50 pods triggers 100 stop-the-world rebalances.
- One malformed record blocks a partition while every other partition is fine, and lag on that one partition climbs to millions.
- A retry strategy that looks innocent silently breaks per-key ordering.

Interviewers use consumer questions to separate people who have *run* Kafka from people who have read about it.

## Core concepts

### The poll loop and its threads

The Java consumer is **not thread-safe** and is designed around one application thread calling `poll()` in a loop:

```java
consumer.subscribe(List.of("orders"), rebalanceListener);
while (running) {
    ConsumerRecords<String, Order> batch = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(500));
    for (ConsumerRecord<String, Order> r : batch) {
        handle(r);                    // your business logic
    }
    consumer.commitSync();            // at-least-once: commit AFTER processing
}
```

`poll()` does much more than fetch data. It also:

- joins or rejoins the group and runs the rebalance protocol,
- invokes your `ConsumerRebalanceListener` callbacks,
- runs auto-commit if enabled,
- proves to the coordinator that the *processing* thread is still making progress.

Separately, a **background heartbeat thread** (since Kafka 0.10.1) sends heartbeats to the coordinator so that *liveness of the process* is decoupled from *speed of processing*.

### Three timeouts you must not confuse

| Config | Default | What it detects | Who enforces |
|---|---|---|---|
| `heartbeat.interval.ms` | 3 s | — (how often the heartbeat thread pings) | client |
| `session.timeout.ms` | 45 s (was 10 s before Kafka 3.0) | **Dead process**: crash, kill -9, network cut, very long GC | coordinator: no heartbeat within this window → member removed |
| `max.poll.interval.ms` | 5 min | **Livelock**: process alive but stuck/slow in your handler | client: if `poll()` isn't called again in time, the client proactively leaves the group |

Rule of thumb: `heartbeat.interval.ms` ≈ one third of `session.timeout.ms`.

```
time ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►

heartbeat thread:  ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥   ♥    (every 3s — process is alive)

app thread:   poll ── handle 500 records ─────────────────────────── … still handling
              │                                                 │
              t=0                              t=max.poll.interval.ms (5 min)
                                                                └─► consumer leaves group,
                                                                    partitions reassigned,
                                                                    uncommitted batch reprocessed
                                                                    by another member
```

The classic failure: `max.poll.records` (default 500) × slow per-record work (say 1 s each) = 500 s, longer than the 300 s poll interval. The consumer is evicted, the batch is redone by someone else, who is *also* too slow — an endless rebalance loop with zero progress. Fixes, in order of preference: make the handler faster (batch DB writes), lower `max.poll.records`, hand work off asynchronously (with careful offset tracking), and only then raise `max.poll.interval.ms` deliberately.

### What triggers a rebalance

- A member **joins**: new pod, scale-up, restart (a restarted consumer gets a fresh member id unless static membership is used).
- A member **leaves**: clean `close()`, missed heartbeats past `session.timeout.ms`, or exceeded `max.poll.interval.ms`.
- **Subscription metadata changes**: partitions added to a subscribed topic, or a regex subscription now matches a new topic.
- Not a trigger by itself: **coordinator failover** (the broker hosting the group's `__consumer_offsets` partition changes). Members rediscover the new coordinator, which reloads the group state; a rebalance only follows if members time out in the meantime.

### Eager vs cooperative rebalancing

**Eager (classic) protocol**: every member revokes **all** its partitions, everyone rejoins, the elected group leader computes a brand-new assignment, everyone resumes. The whole group stops consuming for the duration — "stop the world".

**Cooperative (incremental) protocol** (KIP-429, Kafka 2.4+): members keep everything that isn't moving. Only partitions that actually change owner are revoked, then handed out in a follow-up round. Unaffected partitions never stop.

```
3 consumers, 6 partitions; C3 joins a group of C1, C2

EAGER
  C1: p0 p1 p2  ──revoke all──►  (nothing)  ──assign──► p0 p1
  C2: p3 p4 p5  ──revoke all──►  (nothing)  ──assign──► p3 p4
  C3:                                       ──assign──► p2 p5
  ▲ every partition paused during the rebalance

COOPERATIVE (two rounds)
  round 1: C1 keeps p0 p1, revokes only p2;  C2 keeps p3 p4, revokes only p5
  round 2: C3 gets p2 p5
  ▲ p0 p1 p3 p4 never stopped
```

Whatever the protocol, the `onPartitionsRevoked` callback is your last chance to **finish in-flight work and commit offsets** for partitions you're losing. Skip it and the new owner reprocesses everything since your last commit.

**KIP-848** (the next-generation consumer group protocol, `group.protocol=consumer`, generally available in Kafka 4.0) moves assignment logic onto the broker-side coordinator and makes rebalances fully incremental without the client-side "leader computes assignment" step. With it, `partition.assignment.strategy`, `session.timeout.ms` and `heartbeat.interval.ms` become broker-side group settings (`group.consumer.*`) rather than client configs; the classic protocol is still the client default. Worth one sentence in an interview; the concepts above still apply.

### Static group membership

Set `group.instance.id` (for example to the Kubernetes StatefulSet pod name). Now the coordinator knows that a consumer coming back with the same instance id is *the same member*:

- A restart within `session.timeout.ms` → it gets its old partitions back with **no rebalance at all**.
- A static member's `close()` does **not** send a LeaveGroup, so a quick bounce isn't announced as a departure.
- Trade-off: a member that has *really* died isn't replaced until the session timeout expires, so you trade failover speed for deploy stability. Teams typically pair it with `session.timeout.ms` of 2–5 minutes.

### Partition assignment strategies

| Assignor | How it distributes | Moves on rebalance | Protocol | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `RangeAssignor` | Per topic: contiguous ranges of partitions to sorted members | Can move a lot | Eager | You need co-partitioning (partition N of topics A and B on the same member for joins). Skews when many topics: member 0 gets the "extra" partition of every topic |
| `RoundRobinAssignor` | All partitions across all members, one by one | Reshuffles everything | Eager | Even load, no local state |
| `StickyAssignor` | Balanced, while preserving as much of the previous assignment as possible | Minimal | Eager | Local caches/state, older clients |
| `CooperativeStickyAssignor` | Same as Sticky | Minimal | **Cooperative** | **Default choice** for new deployments |

Since Kafka 3.0 the default `partition.assignment.strategy` is `[RangeAssignor, CooperativeStickyAssignor]`, which keeps using Range but lets you upgrade a group to cooperative with a single rolling bounce that removes `RangeAssignor` from the list. (Clients that don't already list `CooperativeStickyAssignor` need two bounces: first add it alongside the old assignor, then remove the old one.)

### Delivery semantics from the consumer's side

Where you put the commit decides the guarantee:

| Guarantee | Recipe | Failure behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **At-most-once** | Commit, then process (or auto-commit with very fast handlers) | Crash after commit → record skipped forever |
| **At-least-once** | Process, then commit (`enable.auto.commit=false` + `commitSync`) | Crash before commit → record redelivered → handler must be idempotent |
| **Effectively-once (external DB)** | At-least-once + idempotent writes (unique key, upsert, dedup table) — **or** store the consumed offset in the same DB transaction as the result and `seek()` to it on startup | Duplicates become no-ops; or offset and effect commit atomically |
| **Exactly-once (Kafka → Kafka)** | Transactions with `sendOffsetsToTransaction` | See [Transactions & EOS](/learn/notes/kafka-transactions-eos/) |

The "offsets in the DB" pattern looks like this:

```java
// on assignment: resume from what the DB says, not from __consumer_offsets
public void onPartitionsAssigned(Collection<TopicPartition> parts) {
    for (TopicPartition tp : parts) consumer.seek(tp, offsetStore.nextOffset(tp));
}

// per record (or batch), one local transaction:
tx.begin();
applyBusinessChange(record);
offsetStore.save(record.topic(), record.partition(), record.offset() + 1);
tx.commit();
```

Commit sync vs async: `commitSync()` blocks and retries retriable errors; `commitAsync()` doesn't block and doesn't retry (a retry could overwrite a newer commit). A common pattern is `commitAsync` in the loop and one `commitSync` in `finally` / on revoke. Note that the committed offset is the **next** offset to read (`last processed + 1`).

### Poison pills

A **poison pill** is a record that fails *every* time — corrupt bytes, a schema the deserializer can't read, a value that crashes your business logic. Kafka never deletes a record because you failed on it, and a naive loop retries it forever while that partition's lag grows unboundedly.

Defences:

1. **Fail-safe deserialization**: Spring's `ErrorHandlingDeserializer`, or consume bytes and parse inside a try/catch, so a bad record becomes a handled error instead of an exception thrown out of `poll()`.
2. **Classify errors**: *transient* (timeout, 503, lock contention) deserve retries; *permanent* (validation failed, unknown enum, schema mismatch) go straight to the DLQ.
3. **Bounded retries, then DLQ**: after N attempts publish the record (original key/value plus headers such as exception class, message, original topic/partition/offset, attempt count) to a dead-letter topic, then commit past it.
4. **Operate the DLQ**: alert on DLQ rate/depth, give people a tool to inspect, fix and **replay** DLQ records into the source topic.

### Retry strategies

**Blocking (in-place) retry** — retry inside the handler with backoff before moving on.
- Keeps strict per-partition order.
- Stalls the whole partition while retrying; total retry time must stay well under `max.poll.interval.ms` (or use `pause()`/`resume()` on the partition and keep polling).

**Non-blocking retry topics** — on failure, produce the record to a delay tier and move on:

```
orders ──fail──► orders-retry-5s ──fail──► orders-retry-1m ──fail──► orders-retry-10m ──fail──► orders-dlt
   ▲                                                                                              │
   └────────────────────────── manual / tooling replay after fix ◄─────────────────────────────────┘
```

Each retry consumer waits until `record timestamp + delay` before processing (usually by pausing the partition). The main topic never blocks. The cost: **per-key ordering is gone** — event 2 for order 42 can be processed while event 1 is sitting in `orders-retry-1m`. Use it for independent events; for state-machine streams prefer blocking retries or park the *whole key* until the failed record succeeds. Spring Kafka's `@RetryableTopic` builds this topology for you.

**Backoff**: exponential with **jitter** (e.g. `delay = random(0, base × 2^attempt)` capped at a max). Without jitter, every consumer that failed at the same moment retries at the same moment, and the recovering dependency gets hit by a synchronized wave.

### Rewinding: seek, timestamps and resets

APIs on a live consumer:
- `seek(tp, offset)` — next poll starts there.
- `seekToBeginning(tps)` / `seekToEnd(tps)`.
- `offsetsForTimes(map of tp → timestamp)` — returns the earliest offset whose timestamp is at or after the given time; then `seek` to it.

Offline, for a whole group (all members must be stopped):

```bash
kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server b:9092 --group billing \
  --topic orders --reset-offsets --to-datetime 2026-10-20T09:00:00.000 --execute
# other modes: --to-earliest, --to-latest, --shift-by -500, --to-offset 1200
```

When you need it: replaying after a bad deploy, surgically skipping a single poison record, bootstrapping a new read model from the beginning, or the "offsets in the DB" pattern.

**`auto.offset.reset` is not a rewind button.** It's only consulted when the group has **no valid committed offset** for a partition — a brand-new group, or a committed offset that retention already deleted. `earliest` = start at the oldest available record, `latest` (default) = only new records, `none` = throw. For critical pipelines, `none` turns "we silently skipped three days of data because retention expired" into a loud failure.

### Parallelism beyond the partition count

Within one group, a partition is owned by at most one consumer, so partitions cap parallelism. When handlers are slow and you can't (or shouldn't) add partitions:

1. **Key-sharded worker pool inside the consumer.** Poll a batch, dispatch each record to `workers[hash(key) % N]`, so the same key always runs on the same thread and stays ordered. `pause()` partitions when queues fill. The hard part: you may only commit offset X when **every** record up to X is done, so you need per-partition watermark tracking.
2. **Two-stage topology.** A thin first consumer validates/enriches and re-publishes to a second topic with many more partitions; a wider second group does the heavy work.
3. **Confluent Parallel Consumer** — an open-source library that does key-level (or unordered) concurrency over a single partition and handles the offset bookkeeping for you.
4. **Make the work cheaper first.** Batch inserts, bulk upserts, async I/O, a bigger connection pool — often a 10× win with no new moving parts.

### Fetch tuning

| Config | Default | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| `fetch.min.bytes` | 1 byte | Broker holds the fetch until at least this much data is ready… |
| `fetch.max.wait.ms` | 500 ms | …or until this long has passed, whichever comes first |
| `max.partition.fetch.bytes` | 1 MB | Max data returned per partition per fetch |
| `fetch.max.bytes` | 50 MB | Max data per fetch response overall |
| `max.poll.records` | 500 | Max records `poll()` hands your code (does not change fetch size) |

Raising `fetch.min.bytes` to e.g. 64 KB–1 MB cuts request rate and CPU on brokers and clients for high-volume pipelines, at the cost of up to `fetch.max.wait.ms` extra latency when traffic is light. Latency-sensitive consumers keep the defaults. Modern consumers return an oversized first record batch anyway so they don't stall, but keep these sizes consistent with the broker's `message.max.bytes`.

## How it works (one record, end to end, at-least-once)

```
1. Consumer starts → FindCoordinator → JoinGroup → SyncGroup → gets p3, p7
2. onPartitionsAssigned → (optional) seek to DB-stored offsets
3. poll(): fetch from leaders of p3, p7 (long-poll: fetch.min.bytes / fetch.max.wait.ms)
4. for each record:
      try handle(record)                        ← idempotent: upsert keyed by eventId
      on transient error → retry with backoff (bounded)
      on permanent error / retries exhausted → produce to orders-dlt with error headers
5. commitSync(next offsets for p3, p7)          ← committed to __consumer_offsets
6. heartbeat thread keeps pinging every 3s throughout
7. rebalance announced → onPartitionsRevoked → finish in-flight, commitSync → give up partitions
```

## Real-world examples

- **Spring Kafka** ships most of these patterns as building blocks: `DefaultErrorHandler` with `DeadLetterPublishingRecoverer` (blocking retries then DLT) and `@RetryableTopic` (non-blocking retry tiers).
- **Kafka Streams and Kafka Connect** use the same group protocol under the hood; Streams adopted cooperative rebalancing and standby replicas to keep stateful apps available during rebalances.
- **Confluent's Parallel Consumer** exists precisely because "partitions cap parallelism" bites teams with slow, I/O-bound handlers.

## Common pitfalls

- **Heavy work inside the poll loop** with default `max.poll.records` → evicted for exceeding `max.poll.interval.ms` → rebalance storm.
- **Auto-commit with asynchronous processing**: offsets are committed for records a worker thread hasn't finished → crash loses them.
- **No `onPartitionsRevoked` handling**: duplicates on every rebalance.
- **Infinite in-place retry** on a permanent error → one partition stalled forever.
- **Retry topics on order-sensitive streams** → state transitions applied out of order.
- **`auto.offset.reset=latest` on a critical consumer** → after retention outruns lag, data is skipped silently.
- **Eager assignor + no static membership** on a big Kubernetes deployment → every rollout pauses the whole group repeatedly.
- **Sharing one `KafkaConsumer` across threads** → `ConcurrentModificationException`; use one consumer per thread, or a worker pool behind one poller.
- **More consumers than partitions** expecting more throughput — extras sit idle.

## Practice questions

<Practice>

<QA q="What does a consumer group give you, and what happens when there are more consumers than partitions?" level="easy">

A group spreads a topic's partitions across members, so each partition is processed by exactly one member at a time. That gives queue semantics *inside* the group, while a different group reads the full stream independently.

Each partition can have only one owner per group, so consumers beyond the partition count get nothing and sit idle. They still act as hot standbys: if an active member dies, they pick up its partitions in the next rebalance. To go wider you need more partitions or more work done per record.

</QA>

<QA q="Auto-commit vs manual commit — what can go wrong with `enable.auto.commit=true`?" level="easy">

Auto-commit commits the offsets returned by previous polls every `auto.commit.interval.ms` (5 s), at the next `poll()`. It doesn't know whether you actually finished processing:

- If you hand records to another thread, offsets can be committed before the work finishes, so a crash **loses** them.
- If you crash after processing but before the next auto-commit, up to about 5 s of records are **reprocessed**.

Manual commit (`commitSync` after processing) makes the boundary explicit and gives you a clean at-least-once guarantee.

</QA>

<QA q="Explain the poll loop. What is the difference between `session.timeout.ms` and `max.poll.interval.ms`?" level="medium">

One thread calls `poll()` repeatedly. `poll()` fetches records, but it also drives group membership, rebalance callbacks and auto-commit.

- `session.timeout.ms` (default 45 s) is checked by the **coordinator** against heartbeats from a **background thread**. It detects a dead or unreachable process.
- `max.poll.interval.ms` (default 5 min) is the longest allowed gap between two `poll()` calls. It detects a process that is alive but stuck or too slow. When it's exceeded, the client leaves the group even though heartbeats are still flowing.

The classic bug is `max.poll.records` × per-record time going over the poll interval. That causes eviction, then reprocessing, then eviction again. Fix it with smaller batches, faster or batched processing, or async hand-off. Raise the interval only as a deliberate last step.

</QA>

<QA q="What triggers a rebalance, and what happens during one under the eager vs cooperative protocol?" level="medium">

**Triggers:** a member joins (deploy, scale-up, restart) or leaves (close, missed heartbeats, poll interval exceeded); partitions are added to a subscribed topic or a regex subscription matches a new topic.

**Eager:** everyone revokes everything, rejoins, and the leader computes a fresh assignment. The whole group stops in the meantime.

**Cooperative (CooperativeSticky):** members keep partitions that aren't moving, and only the moved partitions are revoked and then reassigned in a second round. Most partitions never stop, which is a big deal for large groups and rolling deploys.

In both cases, use `onPartitionsRevoked` to finish in-flight work and commit, or the new owner will redo it.

</QA>

<QA q="Compare Range, RoundRobin, Sticky and CooperativeSticky assignors." level="medium">

- **Range:** contiguous partition ranges per topic. It co-locates partition N of several topics on one member, which is useful for joins. With many topics it skews, because the first member gets the extra partition of every topic.
- **RoundRobin:** even spread across all partitions, but it ignores the previous assignment, so every rebalance reshuffles everything. That's bad for local caches or state.
- **Sticky:** even spread with minimal movement, but still the eager stop-the-world protocol.
- **CooperativeSticky:** minimal movement plus the incremental protocol. It's the recommended default. Since 3.0 clients default to `[Range, CooperativeSticky]`, so you can migrate with a single rolling restart that drops Range.

</QA>

<QA q="What is static group membership and what problem does it solve? What is the trade-off?" level="medium">

Setting `group.instance.id` gives each consumer a stable identity. Without it, every restart looks like a new member: a leave and a join, meaning two rebalances per pod per rollout.

With it, an instance that comes back within `session.timeout.ms` reclaims exactly its old partitions with **no rebalance**, and a static member's shutdown doesn't send LeaveGroup.

The trade-off is that a member that has really died is only replaced after the session timeout. With the usual 2–5 minute timeout, failover gets slower.

</QA>

<QA q="As a consumer, how do you get at-most-once, at-least-once and effectively exactly-once processing?" level="medium">

- **At-most-once:** commit before processing. A crash loses the record. Rarely what you want.
- **At-least-once:** process, then commit. A crash redelivers the record, so the handler must be idempotent.
- **Effectively-once with an external DB:** at-least-once plus idempotent writes (unique constraint, upsert, dedup table keyed by event id). Alternatively, save the consumed offset **in the same DB transaction** as the state change and `seek()` to it on assignment, so offset and effect are atomic.
- **True exactly-once between Kafka topics:** a transactional read-process-write with `sendOffsetsToTransaction`.

</QA>

<QA q="What is a poison pill message and how do you handle it?" level="medium">

It's a record that fails deterministically (bad bytes, wrong schema, a business-rule crash). Kafka doesn't skip records on failure, so the consumer retries it forever and that partition's lag grows while the rest of the group is fine.

Handling:
- Use a fail-safe deserializer (`ErrorHandlingDeserializer`, or parse bytes in try/catch).
- Classify errors: transient → retry with backoff; permanent → DLQ immediately.
- Bound the retries, then publish to a dead-letter topic with error metadata headers and commit past the record.
- Alert on DLQ depth, and have tooling to inspect, fix and replay from it.

</QA>

<QA q="Blocking retries vs non-blocking retry topics — trade-offs?" level="medium">

**Blocking (in place):** simple and **keeps per-partition order**, but it stalls the partition and can blow `max.poll.interval.ms` unless the retry time is bounded or you `pause()` and keep polling.

**Retry topics** (`x-retry-5s` → `x-retry-1m` → … → `x-dlt`): the main partition keeps flowing and delays are cheap, but **per-key ordering breaks**, because later events for the same key can overtake a record that's waiting in a retry tier. Use them for independent events, not state-machine sequences.

Either way, use exponential backoff **with jitter** so recovering dependencies don't get synchronized retry waves.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you rewind a consumer to a specific offset or point in time? When does `auto.offset.reset` apply?" level="medium">

On a live consumer: `seek(tp, offset)`, `seekToBeginning`/`seekToEnd`, or `offsetsForTimes` (a map of partition to timestamp) followed by `seek`.

For a whole group: stop all members, then run `kafka-consumer-groups.sh --reset-offsets --to-datetime …` (or `--to-earliest`, `--shift-by`, `--to-offset`) with `--execute`.

Typical uses: replay after a bug fix, skipping one poison record, bootstrapping a new projection, or resuming from DB-stored offsets.

`auto.offset.reset` only applies when the group has **no valid committed offset** (a new group, or the offset was deleted by retention). It isn't a general rewind. Use `none` on critical consumers so a retention gap fails loudly instead of silently jumping.

</QA>

<QA q="What do `fetch.min.bytes` and `fetch.max.wait.ms` do? How do they trade latency for throughput?" level="medium">

The broker holds a fetch response until at least `fetch.min.bytes` is available (default 1 byte), or until `fetch.max.wait.ms` passes (default 500 ms), whichever comes first. This is long polling.

Raising `fetch.min.bytes` to 64 KB–1 MB gives fewer, fatter responses and less CPU and network overhead, which is ideal for bulk pipelines. Under light traffic it adds up to `fetch.max.wait.ms` of latency, so latency-critical consumers keep the defaults.

Related sizing: `max.partition.fetch.bytes` (1 MB) and `fetch.max.bytes` (50 MB) cap response sizes. `max.poll.records` only caps what one `poll()` hands to your code.

</QA>

<QA q="How do you increase processing parallelism beyond the number of partitions?" level="hard">

1. **Key-sharded worker pool:** one poller dispatches records to `worker[hash(key) % N]`, which keeps per-key order. Pause partitions on backpressure, and commit an offset only when all earlier records in that partition are done (watermark tracking).
2. **Two-stage topology:** a cheap first consumer re-publishes to a topic with more partitions, and a wider group does the heavy work.
3. **Confluent Parallel Consumer:** key-level or unordered concurrency within a partition, with correct offset management built in.
4. **Make each record cheaper** (bulk writes, async I/O). This is often the best fix, with no extra moving parts.

Adding partitions to a keyed topic is the last resort, because it remaps keys and breaks per-key ordering across the change.

</QA>

<QA q="Commit sync vs async — how would you combine them, and why does `commitAsync` not retry?" level="hard">

`commitSync` blocks until the commit succeeds or fails non-retriably, retrying transient errors. That costs latency on every commit.

`commitAsync` returns immediately and does **not** retry. A late retry of commit(100) could land after a successful commit(200) and move the group backwards.

The common pattern: `commitAsync` after each batch for throughput, and a final `commitSync` in `onPartitionsRevoked` and on shutdown, so the last position is durable.

</QA>

<QA q="A 50-instance consumer group stalls for minutes on every rolling deploy. Fix it." level="scenario">

Each pod restart means a leave plus a join, which is two rebalances. With an eager assignor, every one of them stops the entire group: 50 × 2 rebalances is a storm.

Combine these:
- `CooperativeStickyAssignor`, so unaffected partitions keep consuming.
- **Static membership** (`group.instance.id` = pod name) plus `session.timeout.ms` of about 2–5 min, so a bounced pod reclaims its partitions with no rebalance.
- **Graceful shutdown on SIGTERM:** stop polling, finish the in-flight batch, commit, then `close()`.
- A slower rollout (`maxUnavailable: 1`, readiness gates) so membership changes don't overlap.
- Warm caches before subscribing, so cold pods don't immediately exceed `max.poll.interval.ms`.

On Kafka 4.x, the KIP-848 protocol (`group.protocol=consumer`) makes rebalances fully incremental on the broker side.

</QA>

<QA q="One partition's lag keeps climbing while all others are near zero. What is going on?" level="scenario">

The skew points at that one partition, not at overall capacity:

- **Poison pill:** the consumer is stuck retrying one record. Check error logs and the committed offset that isn't moving. Fix with DLQ or skip via `seek`.
- **Hot key:** one key (a big tenant, a `null`/`UNKNOWN` default) dominates that partition. Check bytes-in per partition. Fix with better keys, composite sub-keys, or routing whales to a separate topic.
- **Slow owner:** the instance that owns it is degraded (GC, noisy neighbour, slow downstream shard). Check per-instance metrics.

Adding consumers doesn't help, because the partition can only have one owner.

</QA>

</Practice>

## Quiz

<Quiz>

<MCQ q="A handler is alive but takes 8 minutes on one batch, with default settings. Which mechanism removes the consumer from the group?" options={["session.timeout.ms, because heartbeats stop", "max.poll.interval.ms, because poll() is not called again within 5 minutes", "heartbeat.interval.ms expiring", "auto.commit.interval.ms"]} answer={1}>

Heartbeats run on a background thread and keep flowing, so the session timeout is not hit; the 5-minute max.poll.interval.ms detects the stuck processing thread and the client leaves the group. The other two are intervals, not failure detectors.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="What are the default values of session.timeout.ms and heartbeat.interval.ms in Kafka 3.0 and later?" options={["10 s and 3 s", "30 s and 10 s", "45 s and 3 s", "5 min and 45 s"]} answer={2}>

Kafka 3.0 raised session.timeout.ms from 10 s to 45 s; heartbeat.interval.ms stays 3 s. 5 minutes is max.poll.interval.ms.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="What is the default partition.assignment.strategy since Kafka 3.0?" options={["RangeAssignor plus CooperativeStickyAssignor, using Range until a rolling bounce removes it", "CooperativeStickyAssignor only", "RoundRobinAssignor only", "StickyAssignor plus RangeAssignor"]} answer={0}>

The default list uses RangeAssignor but already includes CooperativeStickyAssignor, so one rolling bounce that removes Range upgrades the group to cooperative rebalancing.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="During a cooperative (incremental) rebalance, which partitions are revoked?" options={["All partitions of all members", "Only partitions of the leaving member", "None; assignments never change", "Only partitions that actually move to a different owner"]} answer={3}>

Members keep partitions that are not moving and give up only those being reassigned, which are handed out in a follow-up round. Revoking everything is the eager protocol.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="What is the trade-off of static group membership (group.instance.id)?" options={["Offsets can no longer be committed", "A member that has really died is only replaced after session.timeout.ms expires", "It disables cooperative rebalancing", "It requires one partition per consumer"]} answer={1}>

A bounced pod reclaims its partitions without a rebalance, but a truly dead member's partitions wait for the session timeout. It does not affect commits, assignors or partition counts.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="When does auto.offset.reset take effect?" options={["On every consumer restart", "Whenever a rebalance happens", "Only when the group has no valid committed offset for a partition", "When max.poll.interval.ms is exceeded"]} answer={2}>

It applies only to a new group or when the committed offset is invalid, for example deleted by retention. It is not a rewind control; use seek or --reset-offsets for that.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="Why does commitAsync not retry failed commits?" options={["A late retry of an older offset could overwrite a newer successful commit and move the group backwards", "Async commits are always successful", "The broker rejects retried commits", "Retries would trigger a rebalance"]} answer={0}>

Retrying commit(100) after commit(200) succeeded would rewind the group. The usual pattern is commitAsync in the loop plus commitSync on revoke and shutdown.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="What is the main cost of non-blocking retry topics compared with blocking retries?" options={["The main partition stalls during retries", "Records are lost after the first failure", "They require more partitions on the main topic", "Per-key ordering is lost, because later events can overtake a record waiting in a retry tier"]} answer={3}>

Retry topics keep the main partition flowing, but event 2 for a key can be processed while event 1 waits in a delay tier. Blocking retries are the ones that stall the partition.

</MCQ>

<MCQ q="One partition's lag climbs while all others stay near zero. Which fix is least likely to help?" options={["Routing a hot key to a separate topic", "Sending a poison pill to a DLQ", "Adding more consumers to the group", "Investigating the slow instance that owns the partition"]} answer={2}>

A partition has only one owner per group, so extra consumers cannot share its load. Skew points to a poison pill, a hot key or a degraded owner, which the other options address.

</MCQ>

## TL;DR cheat sheet

- One thread, one `KafkaConsumer`, a `poll()` loop. Heartbeats run on a background thread.
- `session.timeout.ms` (45 s) detects a dead process. `max.poll.interval.ms` (5 min) detects a stuck handler.
- Commit **after** processing, and make handlers idempotent.
- Rebalance triggers: join, leave, timeout, new partitions / new regex matches. Commit in `onPartitionsRevoked`.
- Use `CooperativeStickyAssignor` plus static membership (`group.instance.id`) to avoid rebalance storms.
- Poison pill → safe deserializer, error classification, bounded retries, DLQ with metadata, replay tooling.
- Blocking retry keeps order. Retry topics keep flowing but break per-key order. Always back off with jitter.
- `seek` / `offsetsForTimes` / `--reset-offsets` rewind. `auto.offset.reset` applies only when no committed offset exists.
- Past the partition ceiling: key-sharded workers, two-stage topics, Parallel Consumer, or cheaper work.
- `fetch.min.bytes` up means more throughput and slightly more latency.
